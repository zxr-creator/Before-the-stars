## From early communities to urban civilization

The standard start contains gathering, hunting, simple tools, and temporary or semipermanent settlements. It has no prebuilt state bureaucracy, formal religion, universal literacy, metal industry, modern market, or capitalism. Agriculture, permanent settlement, and cities are capabilities to develop. A UI “civilization seat” does not imply they already exist. An initial seat represents connected communities; its actual control covers organized camps, members, and resource-use rights, not taxable territory created by coloring a map.

Shared reserves, reciprocal gifts, and collective labor precede monetary markets. Authority over common inventories and organized labor comes from existing customs and participation; government cannot summon all residents' property. Early projects settle in actual goods, qualified labor, and time. A project without a monetary fee still consumes these inputs. Without monetary institutions, cash accounts and budget ceilings may be zero. Construction cannot be permanently blocked by an inability to pay wages in nonexistent money.

Reuse `start_project`, `set_production`, and known policies to declare in-kind inputs and organizational arrangements; do not introduce unlimited labor requisition. Private production first reserves subsistence and care, then participates through accepted collective obligations, reciprocity, or compensation. Coercive mobilization separately requires executable organization and creates resistance, flight, and supply consequences. Money, commodified labor, and fiscal collection become available through known institutional adoption, then use the established market and cash ledger. Early intercommunity trade can use paired in-kind delivery clauses with designated goods in escrow. An omniscient universal market price cannot automatically convert them. Without a trusted custodian, parties may exchange in batches or accept first-delivery risk.

People may remain distributed among many camps; cities are not the only valid way to live. They become principal administrative, exchange, educational, and specialized production centers through sustained surplus, transport, and organization. Starting content and tutorials describe existing capabilities only, without listing future modern projects.

## Settlements and hinterlands within a region

Retain 24 or 48–72 strategic regions, with bounded settlement nodes and land-use accounts rather than thousands of manually managed tiles. The first version budgets up to 4 aggregate nodes per region: central settlement, rural group, extraction camp, and another specialized settlement. These are capacity budgets, not four mandatory building slots. New actual settlements may join a group with equivalent transport and service conditions. When equivalence is impossible, retain the difference and report the object-budget issue rather than silently removing minority residents. Industry may be urban, rural, or at a mine; factories are not restricted to cities.

| Unit | Main functions | Required costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Camps and rural groups | Food, timber, basic crafts, household care, and ecological maintenance | Land, water, labor, storage, and transport to centers |
| Urban centers | Markets, administration, specialized production, schools, and medical services | Imported supplies, housing, sanitation, maintenance, transport, and organizers |
| Extraction camps | Mineral/fuel extraction and preliminary processing | Reserves, equipment, skilled labor, drinking water, food, transport, and occupational health |
| Industrial clusters | Processing, repair, instruments, and equipment manufacture | Materials, energy, skills, storage, emissions treatment, and markets |

Each person resides at one node or is in transit. Commuting consumes time and transport capacity. Urban registration cannot also provide free full-time rural, mining, and military labor. Elementary teaching, care, and administrative services have reachability limits; a school somewhere in a region does not serve every village beyond a mountain.

Land accounts record use and ownership: cultivation, grazing/gathering, housing, production facilities, infrastructure, and unused land. Urban expansion consumes land and water; mineral value does not make the same entire surface simultaneously cultivable. Conversion requires rights, construction, relocation, and time. Ecological damage and restoration obligations persist across months. Rural residents may combine occupations, but all activities share one time account.

Local logistics also settle. Short trips arriving within the month still consume time, capacity, and losses; longer trips enter transit. Early transport uses walking, pack animals, and waterways, not free roads that have yet to be discovered. When a center and hinterland share a regional market, quotes include actual last-mile delivery costs. Cross-region transport retains its minimum one-month rule.

## Strategic choices in joint planning

Cities may attract rural labor and increase manufacturing while reducing the next food output. Imports can cover the gap but create dependence. Mines supply fuel while potentially competing with irrigation for water. Environmental treatment and rerouting cost resources; a policy sentence cannot remove an externality. Expanded education withdraws current student and teacher time, while a later receiving country may gain the resulting skills through migration. Rural organizations, landowners, industrial organizations, and urban residents make claims based on actual income, services, and rights. They are not four permanent factions.

The agent must decide where production occurs, who lives there, how transport works, who pays, and when benefits arrive. Selecting the recipe with the highest output value is insufficient. Concentration shortens collaboration distances but concentrates water, logistics, and military risks. Dispersion improves resilience while duplicating service and transport costs. There is no rule requiring every region to develop evenly or a mysterious automatic penalty based on city size.

## Education accumulates capability

The four capability domains, complete course templates, teacher/student hours, effective months, practical verification, and transmission rules are in [19](19-education-and-transmission-v0.6.md). Its continuous credential-readiness arithmetic is superseded by the event-driven states in [32](32-population-and-education-v1.0.md). Urban–rural access, receiving capacity, and human time constraints remain. Ancient or medieval labels do not impose nationwide low intelligence or arbitrary literacy ceilings.

Groups retain a joint compressed distribution of expression/literacy, calculation/recording, occupational proficiency, and research methods, with actual course participation, practice, and authorization records. Oral transmission, observation, imitation, and apprenticeship precede writing and scientific method; modern research training cannot open early. Military organization and equipment use separately require relevant occupational skills, training, and command systems. National literacy cannot substitute for them.

Effective monthly teaching is limited by the scarcest qualified teacher time, available student time, space, materials, understandable language, and physical access. Learning also needs nutrition and actual practice. Qualification follows completed training and verifiable tasks, not a single attendance event. A school cannot create qualified teachers from nothing, although experienced craftspeople may teach apprentices within their real competence.

Education's technological limit constrains what can be reliably taught. Without relevant knowledge, qualified mentors, and practical equipment, money cannot open an advanced engineering course. Basic literacy, oral transmission, calculation, and existing craft coverage may still expand. A bottleneck in one advanced course does not stop everyone learning. Unknown capabilities require experiments and research; teachers cannot be assumed to know them beforehand. Conversely, trainees may participate in research that expands future curricula, avoiding a technology–education deadlock.

Education and application are separate. Graduation does not automatically raise output: application needs equipment, organization, inputs, and demand. Underused graduates can change jobs, continue learning, migrate, or temporarily retain unused skills. Education has durable and transferable value; current unemployment does not make all training wasted. Persistent overexpansion costs timing, maintenance, and withdrawn labor rather than giving graduates fictional negative attributes.

Repeating an already mastered course does not stack degrees or raise skills indefinitely. Courses reconcile actual capability and learning history; changing schools, names, or paying departments creates no new knowledge. Refresher training may restore authorization or address actual deficits, but cannot exceed the course's scope.

Example: 50 candidate students, teacher capacity 20, space 30, and required practical capacity 12 permit at most 12 effectively trained people this month. If allocable teaching services cost 2c per learner with no other existing fixed expenses or new construction, an 80c ceiling spends only 24c and leaves 56c. Increasing teacher capacity to 40 still cannot exceed 12. Signed wages and maintenance for new staff and facilities remain payable and consume resources available elsewhere. The choice is whether to supply practice equipment, improve production, or delay expansion, not always maximize the education slider.

Teacher instruction competes with research; student learning competes with farming; soldier training competes with immediate combat. Accepted private labor contracts and public education assignments resolve together during allocation. Separate systems cannot each consume the same person's whole time. Education changes skills and contact channels without necessarily changing religion, politics, or openness.

Practice can become outdated after disuse. In 1.0, scheduled review, supervised work, restoration, and dormant-scope records represent this without monthly decay on every credential. Retained experience, archives, textbooks, and machines make recovery easier but cannot replace every practiced skill. Death and migration remove the associated skilled population; documents, facilities, and human capabilities persist separately. Aggregate training provenance replaces individual teacher–student genealogy.

Education also interacts with expected childrearing standards, employment opportunities, and care time, but there is no direct rule that more education means fewer births. Age structure, income security, services, and family preferences interact; education and public care may reduce existing burdens. The development hypotheses in [12](12-population-and-development.md) remain useful, while [32](32-population-and-education-v1.0.md) defines the current smaller implementation. Causal weights remain referee-private.

## Three speeds and disruptive events

| State | Response timescale | Typical phenomena |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behavior and short-term confidence | Months to years | Avoiding travel after disaster, leaving training during shortages, changing consumption |
| Skills and organizational routines | Years | Skilled workers, reliable collection, teaching capacity |
| Cultural norms and intergenerational transmission | Decades to generations | Durable judgments about public obligations, revising knowledge, and occupations |

Use `SimMonth` throughout. Initial cultural parameters limit normal monthly learning to 20 basis points of the distance toward a target, not a direct 20-point addition, with normal annual drift separately capped at 150 basis points. After major shocks, learning may rise to 60 basis points monthly for at most 12 consecutive months, with a 500-basis-point annual cap. Trait adoption/exit respectively require at least 36/60 months of sustained evidence. These are private, versioned initial design values requiring calibration. Shocks first change confidence and agendas, then behavior. A single defeat cannot rewrite long-term culture, and unexposed populations do not change automatically.

Drift limits follow actual population lineage. For each attribute, sum absolute changes over the past 12 months; opposite changes do not cancel to replenish the allowance, and a new turn does not reset it. Renaming, branching, and merging cultural versions preserve source histories and allowances. Migration can quickly change local population composition without changing existing residents' beliefs. Do not block real migration or falsify composition to keep a smooth graph. Institutions and emergency policy may change before long-term cultural labels satisfy their evidence periods.

Deduplicate high-impact shocks by actual event cluster and known responsibility. Repeated small conflicts, cancelling/restarting projects, and successive propaganda messages neither reset accelerated-learning windows nor stack annual caps. Disaster may produce mutual aid and reconsideration, or avoidance and scapegoating. Direction depends on locally supported interpretations, viable alternative organizations, and observed outcomes; every catastrophe does not guarantee an “awakening.”

Technology content and project milestones may be compressed for prototypes, but an 80-year primitive-to-FTL demonstration cannot be presented as historical chronology. The 1.0 candidate standard spans 240 simulated years at 12 months per decision round and still explicitly compresses technological history. Age, courses, logistics, and governance windows retain their real simulation-month semantics. Historical-paced scenarios would need a longer, separately calibrated span. Longer decision windows still execute monthly with standing procurement, training, and emergency policies based on local information. Changing the calendar requires coherent calibration, not merely accelerating technology and claiming thousands of years were reproduced.

## Private data and acceptance

Conceptual records are `SettlementNode`, `LandUseAccount`, `LocalAccessNetwork`, `EducationProgram`, `SkillDistribution`, and `CulturalMemory`; [32](32-population-and-education-v1.0.md) specifies the effective population blocks, training batches, and authorization states behind them. These are semantic specifications, not implementation or transport code. Nodes, land, inputs, enrollment/completion, and sourced reports may be exposed by permission. Latent culture, learning rates, religious behavioral weights, and adoption thresholds remain hidden.

Verify that a primitive start without cities or money can survive and organize real construction; withdrawing rural labor reduces its available food production; one student cannot simultaneously study and mine full-time; city services do not automatically reach inaccessible villages; equal populations with different skills, religious obligations, organization, and logistics mobilize differently; city destruction, renaming, and migration cannot erase learning history, educational investment, or land damage; and cultural traits do not alternate every neighboring month.
